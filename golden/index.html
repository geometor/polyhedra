Golden Proportions in Polyhedra¶

Exact Algebraic Embeddings of the Divine Proportion across Regular and Dual Solids

Una ipsa eademque convenientia est proportionis divinae qua corpus viginti basium pentagonalium et corpus duodecim basium triangularium conformantur.

The very same correspondence of the divine proportion shapes both the body of twenty pentagonal bases and the body of twelve triangular bases.

—Luca Pacioli, De Divina Proportione, Cap. XXVII (Venice, 1509)

The Golden Ratio in Three Dimensions¶

The golden ratio (the divine proportion) \(\phi = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}\) is the fundamental algebraic root of the quadratic equation:

\[\phi^2 - \phi - 1 = 0 \iff \phi^2 = \phi + 1 \iff \phi^{-1} = \phi - 1\]

While in two dimensions \(\phi\) manifests in the regular pentagon, the golden rectangle, and logarithmic spirals, in three-dimensional constructive geometry \(\phi\) serves as the indispensable structural bridge between the Platonic solids, their compounds, and their duals.

In particular, the regular icosahedron \(\{3, 5\}\) and regular dodecahedron \(\{5, 3\}\) cannot be coordinated over the rational field \(\mathbb{Q}\), but require the quadratic extension field:

\[\mathbb{Q}(\phi) = \{ a + b\phi \mid a, b \in \mathbb{Q} \}\]

In the GEOMETOR architecture, all coordinates, quadrances, face normals, and dihedral spreads are certified symbolically with zero floating-point approximations, guaranteeing 100% exact algebraic truth.

Golden Invariants across the Platonic Solids¶

The Platonic solids exhibit profound internal and mutual golden relationships:

  1. The Icosahedron’s Three Orthogonal Golden Rectangles: The twelve vertices of the regular icosahedron form the vertices of three mutually perpendicular golden rectangles of aspect ratio \(1 : \phi\). The diagonals of these rectangles intersect at the center of the solid and equal the circumdiameter of the icosahedron.

  2. Continuous Morphism from Octahedron to Icosahedron: Dividing the twelve edges of an octahedron of scale \(\phi^2 = \phi + 1\) in extreme and mean ratio (\(1 : \phi\)) generates the exact twelve vertices of the regular icosahedron. When the division parameter \(\tau\) varies from \(1/2\) to \(\phi^{-1}\), the solid smoothly morphs from the Archimedean cuboctahedron into the Platonic icosahedron.

  3. Euclid’s Compound of Five Inscribed Cubes: In Elements Book XIII, Proposition 17, Euclid demonstrates that the twenty vertices of the regular dodecahedron contain the vertices of five distinct regular cubes. The sixty edges of these five cubes partition the sixty pentagonal face diagonals into five disjoint subsets. The ratio of the cube edge quadrance to the dodecahedron edge quadrance is exactly \(\phi^2\).

  4. Kepler’s Rhombic Triacontahedron: In Strena Seu de Nive Sexangula (1611), Johannes Kepler united the twenty vertices of the regular dodecahedron with the twelve vertices of its dual regular icosahedron to form the thirty-two vertices of the rhombic triacontahedron. Its thirty faces are congruent golden rhombi whose diagonal quadrance ratio is \(\phi^2\).

  5. Planar Sections and George Odom’s Theorem: In 1983, George Odom discovered that extending the segment connecting two midpoints of an equilateral triangular face of a tetrahedron to its circumcircle divides the chord in the divine proportion. Similarly, the principal meridian of the icosahedron and the diagonal intersections of the regular pentagon exhibit exact algebraic golden sections.

Golden Polyhedra Theorems¶

Golden Rectangles of the Icosahedron

The twelve vertices of the regular icosahedron \(\{3, 5\}\) partition into three congruent, mutually orthogonal golden rectangles centered at the origin, each having aspect ratio \(1 : \phi\). The six short edges form six edges of the icosahedron, while the long edges cross the solid’s interior. Every circumradius satisfies \(Q_{\text{out}} = \phi + 2\), and the vertical side of each rectangle divides the equatorial radius in extreme and mean ratio.

Golden Division of the Octahedron

If each of the twelve edges of a regular octahedron of scale \(\phi^2\) is directed cyclically and divided in extreme and mean ratio (\(1 : \phi\)), the twelve division points constitute the exact twelve vertices of the regular icosahedron \(\{3, 5\}\). As the division parameter varies from \(\tau = 1/2\) to \(\tau = \phi^{-1}\), the solid smoothly morphs from the Archimedean cuboctahedron into the regular icosahedron.

Five Inscribed Cubes in the Dodecahedron

In Elements Book XIII, Proposition 17, Euclid demonstrates that the twenty vertices of the regular dodecahedron \(\{5, 3\}\) can be constructed by erecting hipped roof wedges upon the six faces of a central inscribed cube. The sixty diagonals of the twelve pentagonal faces partition into five mutually disjoint sets of twelve edges, defining five inscribed regular cubes whose edge quadrance ratio to the dodecahedron edge is identically \(Q_{\text{cube}} / Q_{\text{dod}} = \phi^2\), permuted by the alternating group \(A_5\).

Kepler’s Rhombic Triacontahedron (1611)

In Strena Seu de Nive Sexangula (1611), Johannes Kepler united the 20 vertices of the regular dodecahedron with the 12 vertices of its dual regular icosahedron to form the 32 vertices of the Rhombic Triacontahedron. Its 30 faces are congruent golden rhombi whose long-to-short diagonal quadrance ratio is identically \(Q_{\text{long}} / Q_{\text{short}} = \phi^2\), establishing the divine proportion \(\phi = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}\) as the diagonal aspect ratio of every face.

George Odom’s Golden Construction in the Tetrahedron (1983)

In 1983, the American artist and geometer George Odom discovered that connecting the midpoints \(M_1\) and \(M_2\) of two sides of an equilateral triangular face of a regular tetrahedron and extending the segment to intersect the face’s circumcircle at \(G_2\) divides the chord in the divine proportion: \(Q(M_1, M_2) / Q(M_2, G_2) = \phi^2\), proving that \(\phi\) is an organic invariant of tetrahedral geometry.
